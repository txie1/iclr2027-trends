The conference more than doubled

Submissions and primary areas

ICLR went from 19,814 to 42,822 submissions in one year. Robotics and systems grew fastest and generative models slowest, so every comparison on this page uses shares of the conference.

2026 outcomes and 2027 statusaccepted, withdrawn, desk-rejected
  • 2026 for reference: 5,351 accepted (27% of all submissions), 8,349 rejected, 5,206 withdrawn, 908 desk-rejected.
  • 2027 so far: 1,701 desk-rejected (4.0%) and 292 withdrawn. Most withdrawals come after reviews, so this will rise.
  • Placeholders: a handful of 2027 entries have titles such as “xxx” or “111”. They stay in the counts; too few to move any share.

Chapter 4 · Crossovers

Which ideas travelled to other areas

A paper is filed under one area, but the ideas in it often come from another. This year LLM agents, world models and on-policy distillation travelled furthest, and LLM efficiency tricks moved into video and robot models.

Not more borrowing overall. The share of papers using at least one tracked idea from outside its main areas was 56% in 2026 and 50% in 2027. What changed is which ideas travel.

Two ideas from different families in the same abstract, ranked by growth in papers using both. Every pair grew faster than its two ideas did on their own. “A → B” means A applied to B.

How crossovers are countedmain areas, rankings, what to distrust
  • Ideas. 81 tracked terms from the trend board (chapter 7): fixed text patterns over title plus abstract. Model names, benchmark names and three generic terms (language model, reasoning, transformer) are left out. A mention is not the same as use; some abstracts mention an idea only in passing.
  • Areas. The primary area authors chose at submission, grouped into the 12 areas of chapter 2. Each paper has exactly one.
  • Main areas. For each idea, the area with the most papers using it in 2026, plus the 2027 leader if different (2027 only for ideas with under 40 papers in 2026). A crossover is an idea used in any other area. The map marks main areas with a ring.
  • The opening chart. Ideas are ranked by papers in non-main areas beyond what those areas’ own growth would give: for each area, 2027 papers minus 2026 papers times the area’s growth. It shows the top eight ideas (or fourteen) and, for each, the three largest gains with at least 20 papers in 2027.
  • What each area borrows. Ideas outside their main areas with at least 15 papers in the area in 2027 and under 5% of the area in 2026, ranked by change in share (adding 3 papers to each year to damp small counts).
  • Ideas that combine. Two ideas in the same abstract. Both had at least 40 papers in 2026, they are not variants of one family (GRPO and RL, say), they share at least 25 papers in 2027, and the pair grew at least as fast as the two ideas would predict on their own. Ranked by change in the share of submissions using both, at most three pairs per idea. Arrows (A → B, A applied to B) were set by a model reader from the example titles.
  • Examples. Papers whose title names the idea come first, then a fixed random order. They illustrate the count; they are not picked for quality.
  • Notes. A model reader wrote the short descriptions from the example titles behind each idea, area and pair (3 to 15 titles each), so they describe typical papers, not every paper. All counts come from the data.

Chapter 5 · Models

The model zoo turned over

Qwen went from 0.74% of submissions in 2025 to 8.7%, overtaking Llama. GPT-5 and Claude replaced GPT-4 as the frontier systems papers name.

Benchmarks moved with them. Reasoning benchmarks fell (AIME 0.92% → 0.47%, GPQA 0.38% → 0.21%), while agent and code benchmarks rose (SWE-bench 0.19% → 0.62%, tau-bench 0.02% → 0.12%). All named benchmarks are on the trend board →

Chapter 6 · Style

The abstracts sound different this year

The stock words of the GPT-4 era collapsed in one year. A new set, about controls and exact effect sizes, replaced them in every area at once.

How to read thisa change in tools more than topics
  • Tools, not topics. The change is abrupt, uniform across areas and concentrated in words that carry no topic. That fits a change in writing tools better than a change in research.
  • Old signature out. The 2025 buzzwords (novel, leverage, seamless, showcase) were the known signature of earlier assistants and faded as authors and tools learned to avoid them.
  • New signature in. The new set (matched, frozen, held-out, percentage points, held fixed, pre-registered) reads like a newer assistant’s idea of rigor.
  • Some real substance. 34% of abstracts now give an exact % figure (up from 23%), more name seeds and controls, and a genre of controlled-measurement papers has appeared (chapter 3.5).
  • For readers. Generic hype words no longer separate weak papers from strong ones, and the vocabulary of control does not guarantee the controls were run.
  • Caveat. Text alone cannot show who or what wrote an abstract; these numbers describe the corpus, not any paper.

Method

How this was made

OpenReview metadata for every submission since 2024, fixed text patterns, and model readers working from samples.

Data, measures and what to distrust
  • Data. Every ICLR submission for 2024 to 2027 from the OpenReview API, including accepted, rejected, withdrawn and desk-rejected papers: 7,405, 11,672, 19,814 and 42,822. Titles, abstracts, author keywords and primary areas only; no PDFs. 2027 data was pulled on 26 October 2026, during reviews.
  • Shares, not counts. The conference doubled, so every trend is a share of that year’s submissions. “×2” means a topic’s share doubled.
  • Topic patterns. Each tracked topic is a regular expression over title plus abstract (shown on the trend board, chapter 7). Patterns favour recall; the ideas in chapter 3 say how much of a 65-paper sample was on topic (45% to 92%). The steering and mechanistic-interpretability patterns were narrowed to drop other senses of the words (steering a vehicle or the verb “steer”; quantum and electronic circuits).
  • Broad words. “LLM” and “reasoning” are distorted by the style change: 2026 abstracts often opened with a stock sentence about LLMs, 2027 abstracts rarely do. Specific terms (GRPO, KV cache, world model) are more reliable.
  • Style lists. Old: novel, innovative, advancement(s), underscore, crucial, remarkable, comprehensive, leverage, enhance, seamless, intricate, pivotal, paving the way, significant challenge(s), showcase, delve, notably, state-of-the-art, superior performance, nuanced. New: percentage point(s), matched, frozen, held-out, strongest, held fixed, seed(s), ask(s) whether, audit, unchanged, control(s), pre-registered, prespecified, survive(s), keeps, stays, leaves, rather than, reaches, “Together,”. The new list holds the words whose share rose most from 2026 to 2027.
  • Reading. Idea descriptions and area briefs were written by model readers from samples (65 matching abstracts per idea, 110 random abstracts per area), using only what the abstracts say. Paper ids and titles were checked against the data.
  • Links. Example papers link to their OpenReview pages. Reviews are double-blind and still running, so the page names no authors and links no preprints, and desk-rejected or withdrawn papers are counted but not listed.
  • Names. Terms are written “full name (ABBR)”. Tight spots such as chart labels use the abbreviation alone, with the full name on hover.
  • Merged terms. Overlapping tracked topics were merged (RSI into self-improvement, world-action models into world models, looped models into latent reasoning, “agentic” into LLM agents). For the explorer and the term counts in area briefs, variants count as one term: singular and plural, spelling (finetuning, fine-tuning, time series, time-series), abbreviations with their full names (SFT, T2I, KV cache, PDE and others), near-synonyms reviewed term by term (tool calling and tool use), and generic endings (“reinforcement learning algorithm” counts as reinforcement learning). TTS (test-time scaling or text-to-speech), SSL (self- or semi-supervised), SAM (segment anything or sharpness-aware minimization) and MAE (masked autoencoder or mean absolute error) stay unmerged because they are ambiguous.
  • Explorer. Its terms are the keywords authors chose: 2,384 terms used as a keyword at least 8 times across the four years and found in at least 15 titles or abstracts in some year, reviewed term by term by a model reader to merge variants and remove generic entries. Counts are documents whose title or abstract uses the term, and a phrase never spans punctuation. Other single words can be searched but are not suggested.
